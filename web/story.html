Chapter 1 · A day in a shopper's pocket

Shopping that knows you. Even offline.

A ShopSmart shopper opens the app in the gaps of the day: on the train, in the parking garage, halfway down aisle 12. Those are the moments a personalized cart converts, and they are the moments the phone has no signal.

Subway tunnels

Twenty minutes each way with nothing to do but reorder diapers. Zero bars between stations.

Parking garages

Two levels below the office. The cart she built at lunch won't submit until she reaches the ramp.

Deep in the store

Steel shelving and a concrete roof. "Which aisle is it in?" is asked exactly where the answer can't load.

Rural roads

Hudson Valley: forty minutes between town and home, most of it a dead zone.

Airplane mode

The weekly flight is when the household order gets planned. It should still be priced right.

Basement apartments

Wi-Fi that drops the moment a checkout form posts. The order is lost, and so is the sale.

Friday, 8:10 am - the Blue Line, somewhere under Boston

Maya Chen opens ShopSmart between stations. Her smart cart is already there: the next-basket model scored it at 4:30 am and it reached her phone before she woke up. Coffee pods run out tomorrow, diapers in three days. She adds the stand mixer she has looked at four times, and the app tells her a competitor has it for $26 less. She checks out with a price match: $512.90, priced on the phone at her Plus tier, store credit applied. The phone says On device. At the top of the station stairs it says Awaiting Customer Care, and before she reaches her desk the order is placed at the matched price, with one box of coffee pods backordered and an ETA on the card.

Talking points. Mobile personalization is an offline problem first and an AI problem second. Every recommendation, price and profile fact the shopper needs is on the phone before they open the app; nothing in the tunnel waits on a network. The AI assistant and the governance come along once the data plane is right.
Chapter 2 · What the app needs

Eight requirements. Four for the phone, four for the AI and the Databricks bill.

On the phone
  • The shopper's whole world, on device. Profile, membership, wallet, smart cart, picks, order history, the catalog and their home store's aisle map, before they open the app.
  • Browse, price and check out with no signal. Member pricing, multi-buy savings, tax, store credit and the purchase-limit check all compute locally.
  • Sync when the radio comes back. Orders go up; confirmations, backorders and approvals come down to the same card the shopper tapped.
  • Only their own data. One shopper's phone holds one shopper's profile. Channels decide what replicates, not the app.
For the AI and the data platform
  • Grounded in today's export, and only in this shopper's profile. Never another shopper's that happens to be semantically close.
  • Repeat questions are cheap. The second shopper to ask "how does a price match work?" costs nothing; the same personal question twice costs nothing.
  • Actions are governed. Orders, price matches and goodwill credits run as audited tool calls; price matches and credits wait for a human.
  • The Databricks bill doesn't grow per tap. One scheduled job a day. No warehouse query per screen, no Model Serving call per session.
Talking points. The last requirement is the one the data platform team cares about. Databricks is excellent at producing the recommendations. It is an expensive place to serve them from, millions of times a day, to phones that are often offline.
Chapter 3 · How it usually gets built

Every screen is a warehouse query.

The obvious design: the app calls an API, the API queries the Databricks SQL warehouse for the shopper 360, order history and pricing, calls Model Serving for recommendations, and calls an LLM for the assistant. It works when there's signal, and the meter runs on every tap.
